title: Attitudes
description: The two dispositions the practices express — test-first and user-first — and why each is a design method rather than a phase.
---

Effective software development embodies two attitudes. They are not steps in a
process and they do not appear on a board: they are the dispositions that decide
*how* every [practice](/doc/practices/) in the next section gets run.

| Attitude | The question it puts first | What it is really a method for |
|----------|---------------------------|--------------------------------|
| [Test-first](/doc/attitudes/test-first/) | How would I know this is wrong? | Designing a component, before it exists |
| [User-first](/doc/attitudes/user-first/) | Who has to live with this, and what does it cost them? | Deciding what is worth building at all |

## Why they are separated from the practices

A practice is a named ritual, with named participants, that produces an artefact.
An attitude has none of those things — it is what a person brings *to* the
ritual, and it is what determines whether the ritual produces something or
becomes ceremony.

[Example mapping](/doc/practices/example-mapping/) run by people who are not
thinking about the user produces rules nobody needed.
[ATDD](/doc/practices/atdd/) run by people who are not thinking about design
produces a test suite pinned to an implementation. The ritual is the same in both
cases; the attitude is what was missing.

## What they have in common

Both are **design methods disguised as verification activities**, and both are
routinely mistaken for a phase owned by somebody else — testing for the quality
department, user experience for the design department.

That mistake is expensive in the same way in both cases: it moves the cheapest
moment to discover a problem to a point after the decisions have been made.
